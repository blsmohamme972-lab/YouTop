Second-stage closure of the ductus arteriosus is complete by 2 to 3 weeks after birth: D.
The first stage is functional: within about 10 to 15 hours the ductal muscle contracts, triggered by the rise in oxygen tension and the fall in prostaglandins as the placenta is lost and the lungs clear them.
The second stage is anatomical: the intima thickens and the duct becomes fibrous, ending as the ligamentum arteriosum.

Forty percent of patients with an untreated PDA will die by age 45: A.
A large duct floods the lungs and strains the left heart; heart failure, pulmonary hypertension and endocarditis take their toll over the years, so even a quiet duct is closed.
In adults the duct is often calcified and fragile, so device closure, or a median sternotomy with bypass, is safer than a left thoracotomy.
The duct must be controlled before cardiopulmonary bypass starts, or the pump's flow will flood the lungs.
Neonatal ductal aneurysms often resolve on their own, and thoracoscopic (VATS) clipping can be used even in premature babies and neonates.

The residual leak rate is higher in large PDAs treated with coil embolization: A.
Coils fill a small duct well, but in a large one blood keeps flowing round them, and more coils raise the risk of protruding into the aorta or the pulmonary artery.
Large ducts are closed with a device such as the Amplatzer duct occluder, which was designed for them.
Very small infants are riskier to close from the groin, and a left-to-right shunt with pulmonary hypertension is a reason to close; only a right-to-left shunt from fixed pulmonary vascular disease forbids it.
Coils and devices can also embolize into the pulmonary arteries, where they are usually retrieved with a snare.
