A symptomatic 90% carotid stenosis together with critical left main and right coronary disease is best treated by synchronous carotid endarterectomy and CABG.

== 13
source: مثل عربيّ
explanation:
After a failed ring repair for ischaemic mitral regurgitation, with a moderate-to-severe central leak and an ejection fraction of 30%, the best step is to go back on bypass and replace the valve with a chordal-sparing bioprosthesis.
Ischaemic regurgitation comes from a dilated ventricle tethering the leaflets, and a ring that has already failed shows the tethering is too strong to repair.
A smaller ring tightens the annulus further but does not release the tethering, and an edge-to-edge stitch adds stenosis without fixing the cause.
Keeping the chordae preserves ventricular shape and function, which matters when the ejection fraction is low.
A central jet is a valve problem, so revising the circumflex graft does not help.

explanation:
A post-infarction ventricular septal defect with cardiogenic shock, two vasopressors, intubation and rising creatinine is best managed first with veno-arterial ECMO.
VA-ECMO restores flow to the organs at once and lets the infarcted septum firm up before the defect is repaired.
Repair in the first days of shock, through friable necrotic muscle, carries a very high mortality and a high rate of residual defects.
A left ventricular assist device alone does not support the failing right side and can draw blood across the defect from right to left.

explanation:
Severe degenerative mitral regurgitation from posterior leaflet prolapse with an ejection fraction of 45% and an end-systolic diameter of 45 mm needs surgery now, even without symptoms.
An ejection fraction of 60% or less, or an end-systolic diameter of 40 mm or more, shows the ventricle is already failing and is a class I indication.
A posterior leaflet prolapse in a healthy 45-year-old is almost always repairable, with excellent long-term results.
Waiting for symptoms or another echo lets the ventricle decline further, sometimes for good.
Transcatheter repair is for patients at high surgical risk, not for a healthy woman.

explanation:
When an acute type A dissection is suspected, the book's answer is a stat CT of the chest and abdomen, then operative repair.
CT is fast, widely available and shows the whole aorta: the entry tear, the extent, the branch vessels and any malperfusion, which guides the operation.
Aortography and MR angiography take too long, and MR is unsafe for an unstable, intubated patient.
A transthoracic echo can show aortic regurgitation and an effusion but cannot rule out or map a dissection.
