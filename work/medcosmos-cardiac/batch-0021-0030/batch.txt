explanation:
A large ventricular septal defect often comes with other lesions, and several of the statements about them are true.
When coarctation of the aorta accompanies a VSD, it usually occurs in infants with large defects who need repair within the first three months of life.
In some patients an aortic valve cusp prolapses into the defect, and aortic incompetence develops over time and progresses.
A patent ductus arteriosus is present in about a quarter of infants with a VSD and congestive heart failure.
So A, B and D are the answer.
Statement C is false: in the United States aortic insufficiency is seen most often with perimembranous defects; doubly committed (juxta-arterial) defects, which carry the highest risk of it, are common mainly in Asian patients.

explanation:
A large VSD is about the size of the aortic valve orifice or larger, not the pulmonary valve orifice, so statement A is wrong.
A large VSD with high pulmonary blood flow returns much blood to the left atrium, which enlarges and shows so on the chest x-ray: statement B is correct.
A small, restrictive VSD lets little blood across, so right ventricular and pulmonary artery pressures and pulmonary vascular resistance stay normal, with no pulmonary vascular disease: statement C is correct.
A pulmonary vascular resistance above 10 to 12 units per square metre is considered a contraindication to operation, because closing the defect would no longer help: statement D is correct.
So B, C and D are the answer.

explanation:
Many VSDs close or shrink by themselves: spontaneous closure occurs in 25% to 50% of patients during childhood, so statement A is correct.
A large VSD floods the lungs, so the infant breathes fast (tachypnoea), feeds poorly and fails to thrive: statement B is correct.
With a large defect and low pulmonary vascular resistance, the extra blood returning to the left heart crosses the mitral valve and causes a mid-diastolic rumble at the apex: statement D is correct.
Eisenmenger's complex means pulmonary vascular resistance so high that the shunt reverses to right-to-left; normal resistance with a left-to-right shunt is the opposite, so statement C is wrong.
So A, B and D are the answer.

story:
في ليلة صاخبة في ساحة التايمز، توقّفت اللعبة الكبرى على الشاشات، وسقط من السماء حجر عملاق من أحجار لعبة التتريس.
كان مصنوعًا من أربع كتل زجاجيّة مضيئة ملتصقة، وعليه رقم مضيء: أربعة، وفي كلّ كتلة مشهد صغير.
ففي الكتلة الأولى جدار من آجرّ انفتحت فيه ثغرة.
وفي الثانية حوت قاتل من البالونات يجلس على جدار راكبًا، نصفه على هذا الجانب ونصفه على ذاك.
وفي الثالثة أنبوب من الدولارات يعتصره ملزم حديديّ كبير حتّى ضاق.
وفي الرابعة غرفة زرقاء انتفخت جدرانها لحمًا أحمر غليظًا.
وجاءت كتلة خامسة تهوي، فيها ساعة مستديرة بعقرب ثوان، تريد أن تلتصق بالحجر.
فلم تجد لها موضعًا، فانزلقت عنه وسقطت وحدها في الضوء البارد.
فصاح الفتى رافي من الرصيف: «أربع كتل تصنع الحجر، والخامسة غريبة عنه!»
فضحك جدّه وهو يرى الحجر يستقرّ، وقال:
quote: «وإن كانوا آلافًا فما هم إلّا واحد، وليس ثمّة إلّا خيالات من يفكّر في العدد.»
source: المثنوي، الكتاب الأول
explanation:
Tetralogy of Fallot has four features, which all follow from the forward displacement of the outlet septum.
The first is a large ventricular septal defect, usually perimembranous and non-restrictive.
The second is an overriding aorta, displaced to the right (dextroposition) so that it sits astride the defect.
The third is pulmonary stenosis, that is right ventricular outflow tract obstruction, often at several levels.
The fourth is right ventricular hypertrophy, which follows the obstruction.
An atrial septal defect is not one of the four, so it is the answer; when it is present, the combination is sometimes called the pentalogy of Fallot.
